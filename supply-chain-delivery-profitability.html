Supply Chain Delivery & Profitability Analysis

An order-level analysis of delivery reliability and profitability designed to answer a practical operations question: where are delays happening, and which shipping choices or markets deserve attention?

01 · Overview

The business question

Supply-chain performance is not only a delivery problem. A shipping mode can affect customer experience, operating reliability, and the profit generated by an order at the same time.

This project evaluates those relationships across shipping modes, markets, regions, departments, categories, and customer segments. The goal is to give an operations team a concise view of where late deliveries and weak profitability overlap.

Key modeling decision: the source data contains one row per product line. Because one order can contain multiple products, counting source rows would overstate the number of orders. I created an order-level analytical table before calculating order-level rates and averages.

02 · Method

How the analysis was built

Data preparation

  • Loaded and audited the DataCo Smart Supply Chain dataset.
  • Parsed order and shipping timestamps and derived shipping delay measures.
  • Grouped line items by Order Id to create one consistent record per order.
  • Validated that order-level status, delivery status, and late-risk values were consistent within each order.

Analysis

  • Measured late-delivery rate, average shipping delay, profit, and loss-making order rate.
  • Compared operational performance by shipping mode, market, and order region.
  • Used SQL queries in DuckDB to produce reusable summary tables.
  • Created focused visualizations to compare reliability and profitability rather than producing a collection of disconnected charts.

Interpretation: Standard Class had the lowest late rate among the four modes in this dataset, while First Class had the highest. That pattern is worth investigating before assuming that a faster service label automatically produces better operational performance.

The findings are descriptive, not causal. Shipping-mode selection may be influenced by destination, product, customer, or order characteristics that are not controlled for in this analysis.

04 · Reflection

What I learned

The most important lesson was that the analytical unit matters as much as the chart. Building the order-level table first prevented line-item duplication from distorting the business metrics.

The next iteration would add a dashboard layer, investigate possible drivers of late delivery, and test whether the observed shipping-mode differences remain after controlling for geography and product mix.
